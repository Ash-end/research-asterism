# Learning a field and constructing a method

Use the full route for a researcher learning a field; select a local node for an existing decision. This is an iterative reading and reasoning path, not a document checklist. The labels below identify reusable moves, not required headings in every answer.

## Preparation — define a tractable question and gather an initial batch

Translate the interest into a tentative phenomenon, target, observable inputs, setting and useful decision. Generate query terms from both problem attributes and possible mechanisms. Use a review or tutorial for vocabulary, then collect primary leads from search and backward/forward citations. Skim a batch of abstracts and introductions at a common depth before deep-reading individually. Record what was actually searched or supplied.

An initial 30–40 abstracts and introductions, narrowed to roughly 20 relevant papers in two or three families, is one experience-based starting heuristic from the teaching inspiration. It is neither a quota nor proof of coverage. With six supplied papers, begin grouping those six; expand when the question or evidence gap calls for it. Do not invent papers to fill a count.

## 1 — find families, features and evolution

Identify (2A) prevalent or influential approaches as reading leads, (2B) families on an explicit mechanism/premise axis, (2C) properties, advantages and limitations, and (2D) the settings in which those properties matter. Start with each author's account of their own method, then compare other papers' criticisms. Keep an unverified criticism attributed to its reporter.

Order papers within a family by time; explain the particular limitation a later change addresses. A later date or similar name does not prove inheritance. Families can overlap; a renamed method with an unknown mechanism stays tentative. Output a compact family map and the next question each family raises. Loop to preparation if a missing term or boundary changes the scope.

## 2 — skim one family and choose a productive reading route

Pick the family most relevant to the current question. Skim body sections to find the clearest explanation, useful survey, accessible worked example, and papers containing decisive details. Read the easy explanation before the difficult core paper when this saves effort. Cross-reading several papers can expose the same premise more clearly than reading every line of one paper in order.

Output a concrete route: which source or section to read first, what it should clarify, and which decisive equation, operation, proof or protocol follows. Abstract-only access limits what can be settled; request or retrieve full text only when needed.

## 3 — explain premises, advantages and their operative cause

For the chosen family examine (3A) whether assumptions are realistic in the target setting, (3B) what advantage is claimed or supported, and (3C) how the key relation or procedure could produce it. Before reading the explanation, make a provisional guess when helpful. Check the source and explicitly revise the guess; preserve a useful partial explanation without presenting the earlier guess as an observation.

Derive a formula's consequence under its actual conditions. If `y=gx+b` and a contemporaneous known zero signal gives `y0=b`, `(y-y0)/g=x` for nonzero known `g`; an initial reference instead leaves `(b_t-b_0)/g`. The unknown timing of the reference is a consequential premise, not permission to assume cancellation.

Do not require full proofs or every algebraic step for all papers. Inspect the dependencies that determine the current claim; unresolved ones remain open. Ask what information is available and whether a failure is due to missing information, model/readout restrictions, implementation or a mismatched target.

## 4 — summarize the family's value and limitations

Summarize the family's evolution, shared mechanism, valid setting, advantages, remaining weaknesses and their causes. Move from setting relevance (2D) to (3D) which weakness matters given actual requirements. Where useful, a richer paper note can cover creative idea, benefit, conditions, limitation, harm, cause, and when to borrow which point (6A–G). Use existing notes instead of creating a required new registry.

Name the source and depth behind each property: reported, derived, measured, or hypothesized. Then return to step 2 for another consequential family, rather than claiming one family's description establishes the whole field.

## 5 — revise the problem and retrieve again

Use the family comparison to refine the target, keywords, and question. Identify what was missed: a prerequisite, alternative name, earlier equivalent method, changed setting or rival interpretation. Search and reading may alternate. New core material returns to step 3; a changed objective may return to preparation. Stop expanding when another source is unlikely to change the immediate decision, while retaining the search boundary.

## 6 — compare method properties with problem requirements

Build two tables only when comparison helps. The method table states properties under conditions, with source reports, conditional derivations, empirical estimates and untested guesses visibly distinct. The problem/application table states which properties matter, thresholds and reasons. A theoretical property can be justified without a measurement; a claimed numerical performance needs actual outcome evidence.

Join by property and compatible conditions, not an average score. Locate a consequential mismatch and the core source behind it. Missing or incomparable evidence is a question to check, not a demonstrated method flaw. If an established method meets the actual need, recommending it can finish the task. See [field map](field-map.md).

## 7 — ask why an advantage or weakness occurs

Trace requirements → limiting premise or failure cause → possibility of relaxing the premise or avoiding the cause. Can an advantage survive weaker assumptions? Does a weakness come from information access, representation, readout, optimization, implementation, evaluation or the objective itself? Check the proposed cause against the mechanism; these categories are possibilities, not a diagnosis from a score.

For cross-domain inspiration, map original difficulty, operative mechanism, conditions and target correspondence. Identify where transfer could fail and what prediction distinguishes it from a rival. A metaphor is a lead, not a guarantee. Use [first-principles reasoning](first-principles.md) as needed.

## 8 — construct a concrete candidate and its first check

Specify the candidate's inputs, state or representation, operations and outputs. Explain which cause it avoids, which advantage it inherits, compatibility conditions, cost, a new limitation, and the unsupported step. An algorithm needs usable steps; a theory candidate needs a statement and dependency route; an interpretation needs a material-grounded explanatory relation. Do not finish at “combine A and B.”

Compare the closest known mechanisms after or during construction. Overlap can reduce the novelty claim without erasing usefulness; an ordinary tool may answer the question. Choose a cheap check with different predictions under the candidate and a credible rival. Define continue, revise, stop this formulation or inconclusive conditions at a level appropriate to the claim. See [idea construction](idea-test.md).

## Feedback and proportional entry

A positive signal leads to the strongest alternative explanation and an informative boundary. An effective negative test can send the method back to step 7, or the problem/requirement choice back to step 6. Correct a source misunderstanding at step 3. Preserve failed predictions rather than rescue them by renaming.

Enter locally when the user already has a method or asks one equation question. A 150-character request for the error of `y/g` does not need a literature roadmap. A mentor can ask a targeted question and show one reasoning move; a collaborator should offer a substantive candidate. No stage gives execution authority beyond the current task.
